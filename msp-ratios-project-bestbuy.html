🛒 Best Buy at the Store — Kalihi Edition

Turn Auntie Malia's Sunday potluck shopping list into a unit-price detective.

🎯 The Assignment

Auntie Malia is hosting her family's Sunday potluck in Kalihi. She has $80, a car, and three stores she can hit on the way home: Times Supermarket Kalihi, Don Quijote on Nimitz, and Costco Iwilei. Every store sells the same items — but in different sizes at different prices. Your job is to build a spreadsheet that figures out which package is the real best buy, and by how much.

Your finished spreadsheet will:

  • ✅ List 9 shopping options across 3 items (Spam, rice, shoyu) and 3 stores
  • ✅ Compute the unit price ($/oz or $/lb) for every option
  • ✅ Star ★ the best-buy option for each item automatically
  • ✅ Show how much more expensive each non-winner is, in %
  • ✅ Sort by unit price and chart the results
  • ✅ Answer the real-life question at the bottom: when is the best unit price NOT the best deal?
💡 The big idea: A unit price is a rate — dollars per one unit. That's exactly what this whole ratios & rates unit is about. You're using division to reduce every option down to the same "per one" scale so you can actually compare them.

3 Write the Unit Price Formula

Now add Column F — Unit Price. This is the heart of the whole assignment. Unit price = price ÷ size. That's a rate: dollars per one unit.

Add Column F
C (Size)D (Unit)E (Price)F (Unit Price)
412oz3.99=E4/C4
525oz6.99=E5/C5
696oz22.99=E6/C6
The unit price formula:   =E4/C4
🧠 What it means: price ÷ size = dollars per one unit
For 12 oz Spam at $3.99: 3.99 ÷ 12 = $0.3325 (per oz)
For 25 oz Spam at $6.99: 6.99 ÷ 25 = $0.2796 (per oz)
For 96 oz Spam at $22.99: 22.99 ÷ 96 = $0.2395 (per oz)

Fill it down

  1. Type =E4/C4 in F4 and press Enter.
  2. Click F4 again. Grab the little blue square at the bottom-right corner and drag down through F12.
  3. Format F4:F12 as Currency with 4 decimal places (Format → Number → More Formats → Custom → $0.0000). Four decimals matter — the differences are pennies!
💬 Want the cell to actually SHOW "/oz" or "/lb" after the number? The plain formula just gives a number. You have two ways to add the unit label:
  • Option A — Custom number format: Format → Number → Custom → $0.0000"/oz". The cell displays $0.3325/oz but the underlying value is still just the number, so the star formula in Step 4 still works. (Use two versions — one for oz rows, one for lb rows.)
  • Option B — Concatenate the unit column: replace =E4/C4 with =TEXT(E4/C4,"$0.0000") & "/" & D4. Now the cell literally contains text like $0.3325/oz. Downside: it's no longer a number, so MINIFS in Step 4 won't work on it. Keep the number-only version in F for math and put the labeled text in a separate display column if you want both.
💡 Why divide? Because you want to know "what does ONE unit cost." That's literally the definition of a unit rate. Every problem in this unit — miles per hour, cost per pound, students per teacher — is the same move: quantity ÷ how many.

4 Star ★ the Best Buy (per Item)

Now the fun part. Add Column G — Best? A formula automatically marks the winner in each item group.

G4:   =IF(F4=MINIFS($F$4:$F$12, $A$4:$A$12, A4), "★ BEST", "")

What MINIFS does: finds the smallest number in one range, but only among rows where another range matches a condition.
🧠 "Give me the minimum of F4:F12, but only among rows where the Item column (A) equals the Item on this row."
📌 That's why the star can jump to a different row for each item — Spam winner, rice winner, shoyu winner — all in one formula.
💡 Test it live: change the Costco Spam price (E6) to $30. Watch the star jump. Change it back — the star jumps back. That's a working decision engine.

5 How Much Worse Is Each Non-Winner? (Column H)

Column H — % more expensive than best. This is where ratios come back in disguise.

H4:   =(F4 - MINIFS($F$4:$F$12, $A$4:$A$12, A4)) / MINIFS($F$4:$F$12, $A$4:$A$12, A4)

Fill down. Format H4:H12 as a percentage with 1 decimal (Format → Number → Percent).

What this is computing: (this option − best option) ÷ best option
🧠 That's the classic percent change formula. In this class, we've been calling it "how much bigger, as a ratio to the original."
Times Kalihi Spam: (0.3325 − 0.2395) ÷ 0.2395 = 0.388 = 38.8% more expensive per ounce.
Don Quijote Spam: (0.2796 − 0.2395) ÷ 0.2395 = 16.7% more expensive.
Costco Spam: (0.2395 − 0.2395) ÷ 0.2395 = 0% — it IS the best.
💡 This is why unit price matters: the $3.99 Spam and the $22.99 Spam look completely different in price. But per ounce the small can costs almost 40% more.

6 Sort & Chart

Sort each item group cheapest → most expensive

  1. Select rows 4 through 12 (all data).
  2. Data → Sort range → check Data has header row off.
  3. Sort first by Column A (Item) A→Z, then by Column F (Unit Price) smallest → largest.
  4. The BEST row for each item is now on top of its group.

Add a bar chart of unit prices

  1. Select columns A, B, and F (Item, Store, Unit Price). Use Ctrl/Cmd-click to select non-adjacent columns.
  2. Insert → Chart → Column chart.
  3. Title: Unit Prices — Auntie Malia's Shopping List.
🎨 Look at your chart. The bar for the same item gets shorter as the package gets bigger. That's the economies of scale pattern in one picture.

🤔 The Real-Life Question

Costco wins every single item. So Auntie Malia should just shop entirely at Costco… right?

Actually, most people in Kalihi don't. Why not?

Answer each of these in a Google Doc or on paper. These are the times when the best unit price is not the best deal:

  1. Auntie Malia's family is 6 people. Costco Spam is a 96 oz pack. If they only eat Spam twice a month, how long before that pack goes bad? Is 96 oz really a "best buy" for her?
  2. Costco requires a $65/year membership. If Auntie Malia would save $8/month by shopping there, is it worth it? Show your math.
  3. Times Kalihi is 4 minutes from her house. Costco Iwilei is 25 minutes plus parking. Gas costs about $0.60/mile and each Costco round-trip is 12 miles. What does the "convenience discount" of Times need to be, per shopping trip, to break even?
  4. Auntie Malia has $80 today. The big Costco rice (25 lb) alone is $34.99. If she buys the biggest of every item — Spam $22.99, rice $34.99, shoyu $9.99 — she spends $67.97 and has $12 left for everything else. Is the "cheapest per ounce" the same as the "cheapest today"? Explain.
💡 Big takeaway: unit price is the way to compare across sizes fairly. But real decisions also weigh storage, freshness, upfront cash, and travel cost. A rate is a powerful tool — not the whole story.

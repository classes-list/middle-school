Bloom’s Taxonomy in Math Class

A framework for growing the depth of student thinking — from recall to creation.

🧠 What Bloom’s Taxonomy Measures

Bloom’s Taxonomy (Benjamin Bloom, 1956; revised by Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) classifies cognitive tasks from simplest to most complex. It answers one question: how deeply is the student being asked to think?

The upper three levels — Analyze, Evaluate, Create — are called higher-order thinking skills. Growing student capacity at these levels is a central objective of Math 6, 7, and 8 in Dr. Park’s classroom.

6. Createdesign, invent, compose
5. Evaluatejudge, critique, defend
4. Analyzecompare, examine, contrast
3. Applyuse, solve, demonstrate
2. Understandexplain, describe, summarize
1. Rememberrecall, identify, list

Higher-order thinking = the top three tiers (Analyze · Evaluate · Create).

🧮 The Same Concept at All Six Levels

The same underlying content can be taught at any Bloom’s level. Here is the area of a triangle written six ways:

  • Remember: State the formula for the area of a triangle.
  • Understand: Explain in your own words why the area of a triangle is ½ × base × height.
  • Apply: Find the area of a triangle with base 6 cm and height 4 cm.
  • Analyze: Two different triangles have the same area. Compare their base and height dimensions and explain what stays constant.
  • Evaluate: Two students disagree on which method (½ bh vs. Heron’s formula) is better for this problem. Which is better here, and why?
  • Create: Design your own real-world scenario where finding a triangle’s area matters, and solve it.

Notice: getting the "right answer" is only step 3 out of 6. The top half of the pyramid requires reasoning about the answer, not just producing it.

🎯 How Dr. Park Grows Higher-Order Thinking

  1. Fluency first, always. Remember-Understand-Apply are the foundation. Without them, higher-order tasks feel impossible. Freckle practice, mini-quizzes, and skill drills build this floor.
  2. Every project reaches the top of the pyramid. Every summative task in Math 6/7/8 requires Analyze, Evaluate, or Create work — that is why they are called summative. Students explain reasoning, defend a method, or design their own solution.
  3. Student self-talk uses Bloom’s verbs. When students self-assess, they name what they were doing: "I was evaluating two methods and choosing the better one." That vocabulary becomes a metacognitive tool.
  4. Wrong-answer analysis. When a student gets an answer wrong, we don’t just correct it — we analyze which step broke down and evaluate whether the reasoning was sound before the arithmetic slipped.

Bloom’s Taxonomy in Math Class

A framework for growing the depth of student thinking — from recall to creation.

🧠 What Bloom’s Taxonomy Measures

Bloom’s Taxonomy (Benjamin Bloom, 1956; revised by Anderson & Krathwohl, 2001) classifies cognitive tasks from simplest to most complex. It answers one question: how deeply is the student being asked to think?

The upper three levels — Analyze, Evaluate, Create — are called higher-order thinking skills (HOTS). Growing student capacity at these levels is a central objective across every course in the MSOS middle school.

6. Createdesign, invent, compose
5. Evaluatejudge, critique, defend
4. Analyzecompare, examine, contrast
3. Applyuse, solve, demonstrate
2. Understandexplain, describe, summarize
1. Rememberrecall, identify, list

Higher-order thinking = the top three tiers (Analyze · Evaluate · Create).

🖥️ Google Sheets: Programming as Higher-Order Thinking

One of the strongest ways we practice higher-order thinking in middle-school math is by having students program their own Google Sheets calculators. Building a working spreadsheet is not a passive skill — it forces students to think at every level of Bloom’s pyramid, especially the top three.

How spreadsheet programming maps to Bloom’s

What the student is doing Bloom’s level
Recognize =SUM, =IF, cell referencesRemember
Explain what a cell reference doesUnderstand
Change a yellow input and read the answerApply
Decompose a real problem into inputs, formulas, and outputsAnalyze
Debug a broken formula; decide which of two setups is betterEvaluate
Design your own calculator from a blank sheetCreate

Why spreadsheet programming is unusually strong HOTS practice

  • Decomposition. Students break a messy real-world problem (compound interest, unit conversion, a monthly budget) into cells: inputs, calculations, outputs.
  • Pattern recognition. They see that every rate formula has the same underlying shape and can be reused across problems.
  • Abstraction. They use cell references like =B5*(1+B6/100) instead of hard-coding numbers, so the whole model recalculates when inputs change.
  • Algorithm design. They sequence formulas so downstream cells depend on upstream ones correctly.
  • Instant feedback loop. A wrong formula gives an immediately visible wrong answer, which triggers debugging — the essence of the Evaluate level. A spreadsheet won’t lie to a student the way a worksheet can.

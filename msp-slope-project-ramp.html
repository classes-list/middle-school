♿ Wheelchair Ramp Designer

Slope + Pythagorean theorem = ADA-compliant ramps that everyone can use.

🎯 The Mission

The Americans with Disabilities Act (ADA) requires wheelchair ramps to have a slope no steeper than 1:12 — meaning 1 unit of rise for every 12 units of run.

Your job: design a ramp for a real entrance with a step-up you can measure. Compute the rise, run, ramp length, and slope in every form (ratio, decimal, and percent).

1:12 = for every 1 unit of rise, there are 12 units of run.

It doesn't matter what unit — inches, feet, or meters — the ratio stays the same:
   1 inch up per 12 inches across
   1 foot up per 12 feet across

A steeper ratio like 1:8 is TOO STEEP for a wheelchair. A gentler ratio like 1:20 takes more space but is easier to roll up.

Engineers use different forms in different contexts:
  • Ratio (1:12) — the standard ADA specification. Easiest to check on paper.
  • Decimal (0.0833) — used in calculators and CAD software.
  • Percent (8.33%) — how road grades and highway signs display slope.
All three describe the SAME slope!

5️⃣ Bonus: The Landing Rule

ADA requires a level "landing" every 30 feet of ramp. If a building has a taller step-up, you need to break the ramp into sections with landings between them.

inches
A 60 in step-up at 1:12 slope needs 60 ft of ramp. That's 2 section(s) of 30 ft or less, plus 1 landing(s) between them.

🎓 What You Learned

Ramp design combines slope (rise/run) with the Pythagorean theorem (to find the ramp surface length). The same 1:12 ratio can be written as 1/12, 0.0833, or 8.33% — three different disguises for the same slope.

Engineers, architects, and city planners use these calculations every day to make buildings accessible. Now you can, too.
